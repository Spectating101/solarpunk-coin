# Fiscal Choke Points

## Transaction-Node Architecture and Operational Fiscal Capacity in Southeast Asian Digital Tax Administration

Christopher Ongko (王新福)  
Yuan Ze University  
Publication candidate · September 2026

## Abstract

Digital-tax debates are commonly organized around rates, bases, nexus, and revenue. Yet those variables become administratively meaningful only after a tax system can identify a legally covered event, connect it to a jurisdiction, assign a bounded duty to an actor, and embed that duty in a repeatable collection or reporting process. Digital commerce makes this prior institutional problem unusually visible because suppliers, platforms, buyers, and payment-capable intermediaries can control different combinations of identity, location, invoicing, payment, transaction confirmation, and market access.

This paper defines a **fiscal choke point** as a legally activated transaction node at which a state assigns a bounded fiscal function to an actor positioned to observe or control a relevant part of the commercial event. Using primary legal and tax-administration sources from Malaysia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and the Philippines, the paper compares seven transaction paths through five architectural components: taxable object, liable node, destination or nexus evidence, transaction rail, and reconciliation power. Two cross-cutting dimensions—**node locus** and **event coupling**—identify who carries the duty and how tightly fiscal action is tied to the transaction.

The analysis adds two tests that move beyond descriptive country mapping. First, within-regime boundary cases show that platform presence alone is insufficient for fiscal-node activation. In Thailand, platform liability is conditional on control of the continuous offer-payment-delivery process; in the Philippines, an e-marketplace is not liable in a cited scenario where payment is made directly to the nonresident provider and is outside marketplace control; in Vietnam, the platform-withholding path is tied to online-ordering and payment functionality; in Indonesia, special collector duties require formal appointment. Second, the paper separates **legal architecture** from **operational capacity**. Public sources reveal different downstream machinery for filing, correction, refunds, audit, appeal, and sanctions, but those procedural layers do not establish administrative effectiveness or justify country rankings.

The contribution is comparative and institutional rather than causal. Third-party reporting, firms as fiscal intermediaries, platform VAT/GST liability, regulatory intermediation, and ASEAN digital-tax comparison are prior art. The narrower contribution is a source-controlled transaction-path framework that identifies where fiscal responsibility is activated, tests the boundaries of that activation, and separates legal node assignment from downstream operational performance.

**Keywords:** digital taxation; tax administration; platforms; VAT; withholding; fiscal capacity; regulatory intermediaries; ASEAN; e-commerce; third-party reporting

## 1. Introduction

A statutory tax rate does not collect itself.

Before a digital transaction can generate administrable revenue, a tax system must answer a sequence of prior questions. What is the legally covered object? Why does the transaction belong to the jurisdiction? Which actor must register, collect, withhold, report, remit, or preserve information? At what point in the commercial process does that duty arise? Which records permit correction, reconciliation, audit, or dispute?

Digital commerce exposes these questions because commercial control is distributed. A foreign supplier may know the customer's billing location. A marketplace may identify the seller and control access to the market. A payment-capable platform may know when payment is accepted. A domestic business buyer may be easier for the tax authority to reach than the nonresident provider. An intermediary may facilitate a transaction while lacking control over payment. Different legal systems can therefore tax similar forms of digital commerce through different institutional nodes.

The conventional digital-tax debate often starts later. It compares statutory rates, thresholds, revenue totals, or broad tax labels. Those are legitimate objects of analysis, but they can hide the administrative architecture that makes the obligation executable. A 10% tax administered through seller self-reporting is not institutionally equivalent to a 10% tax collected by an appointed marketplace, withheld by a buyer, or deducted by a payment-capable platform at transaction confirmation.

This paper asks:

> **How do governments convert commercial observability and transaction control into administrable fiscal responsibility?**

The proposed analytical construct is the **fiscal choke point**: a legally activated transaction node at which a state assigns a bounded fiscal function—such as registration, identification, collection, withholding, reporting, remittance, or record retention—to an actor positioned to observe or control the relevant commercial event.

The term is intentionally narrower than the political language of a “fiscal counterattack” against platforms. It does not imply that private commercial infrastructure is illegitimate, that every platform transaction is taxable, or that the state is entitled to all data visible to an intermediary. It identifies an institutional connection between a legally defined duty and a commercially relevant node.

The underlying mechanism is not new. Employers, banks, firms, merchants, and other organizations have long acted as fiscal intermediaries. Third-party reporting can strengthen tax enforcement because organizations generate records for their own operations (Kleven, Kreiner, and Saez 2016). VAT can create information trails with deterrence and enforcement effects (Pomeranz 2015). OECD guidance explicitly addresses full and partial platform liability for VAT/GST on online sales (OECD 2019). IMF and Asia-Pacific guidance likewise treat vendor collection, platform involvement, registration, and simplified compliance as established administrative tools (Brondolo 2021; OECD, World Bank Group, and Asian Development Bank 2022).

ASEAN comparison is also not novel by itself. Regional government and academic work already maps digital-tax rates, tax types, nexus rules, and collection mechanisms. The contribution here must therefore survive two concessions: **platform fiscal intermediation is prior art, and comparative ASEAN digital taxation is prior art.**

The paper makes a narrower contribution. It reorganizes the comparison around the transaction path and asks three questions that are usually separated.

First, **where is responsibility placed?** A provider, marketplace, appointed collector, payment-capable platform, or domestic buyer can each become the fiscal node.

Second, **what activates that responsibility?** Platform status alone is not always sufficient. Payment control, continuous-process control, transaction class, formal appointment, location evidence, or statutory thresholds can determine whether a node is activated.

Third, **what happens after activation?** Filing, correction, refund, audit, appeal, enforcement, and outcome measurement are analytically separate from the existence of the legal duty.

The empirical comparison covers Malaysia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and the Philippines, but the canonical dataset contains seven transaction paths rather than five country labels. The Philippines, for example, is split into B2C provider remittance, B2B buyer withholding, and a qualifying e-marketplace path. This prevents the analysis from treating an entire national tax system as one homogeneous “digital tax.”

The results support four bounded claims.

1. Fiscal responsibility repeatedly attaches to actors controlling functions relevant to administration—payment, customer location, invoicing, transaction confirmation, seller identification, or market access—rather than following one uniform legal form.
2. **Node locus** and **event coupling** are distinct design dimensions: who carries the duty and when it is performed need not move together.
3. Within-regime countercases show that digital-intermediary presence alone does not automatically trigger liability; legal activation depends on specific control or classification conditions.
4. Legal assignment and operational capacity are separate stages. Some jurisdictions publicly expose detailed correction, refund, audit, appeal, or enforcement machinery, but documentation depth cannot be treated as evidence of superior performance.

The paper does not estimate which architecture maximizes revenue or compliance. It does not pool unlike tax bases, interpret collection totals causally, or rank countries. Its purpose is to define the institutional object precisely enough that those questions can later be studied with defensible outcome data.

## 2. Prior art and the remaining research gap

### 2.1 Firms and intermediaries as tax-administration infrastructure

Modern tax systems do not rely exclusively on self-reporting by atomized individuals. Firms maintain accounts, payrolls, invoices, transaction records, and customer information for commercial reasons, creating records that public authorities can recruit through law. Kleven, Kreiner, and Saez (2016) formalize firms as fiscal intermediaries; their framework explains how organizational records can relax enforcement constraints. Keen and Slemrod (2017) place reporting, withholding, enforcement, rates, and administrative cost inside the broader problem of tax-administration design.

Pomeranz (2015) provides complementary evidence from VAT. Third-party transaction trails alter the information environment and can generate enforcement spillovers. The relevant implication is not that every digital trace improves compliance. It is that **administrative capacity depends partly on how legally relevant information is generated, held, transmitted, and reconciled.**

### 2.2 Platform liability is established prior art

Platform-specific fiscal intermediation is also well established. OECD (2019) describes regimes assigning platforms full or partial VAT/GST liability for facilitated online sales, with administrative concentration as one rationale. Brondolo (2021) analyzes registration and collection systems for imported digital services and low-value goods. The OECD/WBG/ADB Asia-Pacific toolkit provides regional implementation guidance. Aslam and Shah (2017) similarly note that peer-to-peer platforms can complicate small-business taxation while simultaneously creating useful records.

The fiscal-choke-point concept therefore does not claim to discover platform collection. Its contribution is analytical: it identifies the legally activated node and its relationship to the transaction path across heterogeneous instruments.

### 2.3 Regulatory intermediation and platform governance

The framework also sits within broader work on regulatory intermediaries. Abbott, Levi-Faur, and Snidal's RIT model emphasizes how regulators can govern targets through intermediaries that possess information, capabilities, or access. Platform-governance research likewise shows that digital intermediaries can control seller entry, information, recommendations, quality rules, and market access.

Fiscal intermediation is a specific case of this broader governance problem. The state is not merely “taxing a platform”; it is allocating bounded public duties across private nodes with different operational capabilities and legal relationships.

### 2.4 ASEAN comparison is not the gap

Regional comparison of digital taxation already exists. The Philippines National Tax Research Center has examined VAT and VAT-like treatment of digital transactions across ASEAN, including rates, structures, and collection methods. Comparative legal scholarship has also examined digital and over-the-top taxation in Indonesia and neighboring ASEAN jurisdictions.

The remaining gap is consequently not country count. It is **transaction-path resolution**. A country can use different nodes for different transaction classes, and legally similar taxes can rely on different operational rails. Conversely, legally different instruments can share a similar administrative relationship to a platform, buyer, or provider.

The paper therefore compares a narrower layer without asserting that unlike taxes are equivalent.

## 3. Research design and source discipline

### 3.1 Unit of analysis: the transaction path

The canonical dataset uses one row per legally distinct transaction path. The seven current paths are:

1. Malaysia — foreign-provider Service Tax on Digital Services;
2. Indonesia — DGT-appointed PMSE VAT collector;
3. Vietnam — payment-capable platform withholding for covered household/individual sellers;
4. Thailand — foreign electronic-service provider with conditional platform shift;
5. Philippines — B2C nonresident digital service provider;
6. Philippines — B2B domestic buyer/withholding agent;
7. Philippines — qualifying e-marketplace path.

The sample is purposive. It is not a representative statistical sample of ASEAN and is not used to estimate a regional treatment effect.

### 3.2 Five architectural components

Each path is coded through five components:

| Component | Question |
|---|---|
| Taxable object | What sale, service, import, payment, or facilitated transaction is legally in scope? |
| Liable node | Which actor must perform the fiscal duty? |
| Destination / nexus evidence | What makes the transaction belong to the jurisdiction? |
| Transaction rail | How is the duty repeatedly performed—registration, checkout, invoice, withholding, filing, payment? |
| Reconciliation power | What records, reporting relationships, or authority powers permit checking and correction? |

Two additional dimensions are coded separately.

**Node locus** identifies the actor carrying the duty. **Event coupling** identifies how tightly fiscal action is tied to the underlying commercial event. A payment-level withholding duty is more transaction-coupled than a periodic provider return, but that does not make it normatively superior.

### 3.3 Source hierarchy

Country claims are controlled by a primary-source hierarchy:

1. statutes, regulations, decrees, and official legal text;
2. official tax/customs guidance and administration portals;
3. official administrative output on registrations, appointments, or collections;
4. multilateral institutional guidance for comparative context;
5. academic literature for theory and prior art.

Later material controls older procedural wording where necessary. Dates are typed as announcement, issuance, statutory effect, operational start, or reporting period rather than treated as interchangeable.

### 3.4 Inference discipline

The paper does not pool collection series from unlike instruments or regress them on statutory rates. Administrative totals can establish reported scale or the existence of remitters, but they do not identify the causal effect of node choice, tax rate, enforcement, or market growth.

The framework is categorical, not ordinal. No `fiscal choke point score` is constructed. A system with more tightly coupled collection is not automatically more effective, less costly, or more equitable.

## 4. Seven fiscal paths across five jurisdictions

### 4.1 Malaysia: provider responsibility and destination evidence

Malaysia's Service Tax on Digital Services illustrates a provider-centered path supported by administrable destination evidence. The MySToDS framework identifies a Malaysian consumer using combinations of payment facility, Malaysian IP or mobile-country indicators, and residence. A foreign service provider above the relevant qualifying threshold must register and account for service tax through a periodic return structure.

Customs guidance currently linked by MySToDS can bring an online platform into the foreign-service-provider framework where it operates relevant transactions on behalf of another person. The comparative feature is the combination of **provider/platform role, consumer-location evidence, and periodic administration**.

The path is bounded by threshold and instrument scope. Malaysia's low-value-goods regime is legally distinct from SToDS and is not merged into one country-level tax base in this paper.

### 4.2 Indonesia: appointed PMSE VAT collector

Indonesia's selected path activates special VAT collection responsibility through formal designation. DGT-appointed PMSE operators collect VAT, provide commercial proof, remit periodically, and report through DGT systems.

The designation requirement is analytically important. Commercial visibility does not automatically create the selected collector duty; legal appointment activates it.

Administrative evidence shows that the system is not merely statutory. As of 31 January 2026, DGT reported 242 active PMSE VAT collectors, 223 of which had collected and remitted VAT, with cumulative PMSE VAT collections of Rp36.69 trillion. These are administrative facts. They do not identify the causal effect of appointment or the amount that would have been collected under another architecture.

### 4.3 Vietnam: payment-capable platform withholding

Vietnam provides the strongest transaction-coupled case. Decree 117/2025/NĐ-CP, effective 1 July 2025, establishes withholding, declaration, and payment responsibilities for covered household and individual sellers where the platform satisfies the relevant online-ordering and payment functionality.

The duty is tied to the successful transaction/payment event. Decree 68/2026 carries the relevant platform responsibility into the 2026 household/individual tax-administration layer, while Decree 141/2026 amends specified thresholds and provisions without displacing the platform mechanism used for this classification.

The functional condition is part of the contribution. Platform status alone is insufficient: the current framework distinguishes the payment/order-capable path from seller activity outside that functionality, where the applicable self-declaration/payment route remains relevant.

### 4.4 Thailand: provider rail with conditional platform shift

Thailand's VAT for Electronic Service system provides a dedicated nonresident compliance rail. Foreign operators above the threshold register, file, and pay through the VES system.

Platform liability is conditional. Revenue Code section 82/13 and Revenue Department guidance assign the platform VAT responsibility where the platform provides the continuous process from offering the service through receiving payment and delivering the service.

The Thai case therefore gives a direct boundary condition: **platform participation is not equivalent to platform liability**. Responsibility shifts when the platform's control of the commercial process meets the statutory test.

### 4.5 Philippines: three nodes inside one national system

The Philippines demonstrates why the country should not be treated as one row.

For covered B2C digital services, the nonresident digital service provider registers and remits through the VDS system. For specified B2B purchases, fiscal action can shift toward the Philippine buyer or withholding agent. A separate marketplace path applies under statutory control/facilitation conditions.

The regime also operationalizes destination through payment, residence, access, and other reliable information, requiring at least two non-conflicting indicators when available evidence is contradictory.

This produces a split architecture: provider, buyer, and marketplace can each become the relevant node depending on transaction class and control conditions.

## 5. Boundary cases: when an intermediary is *not* the fiscal node

A framework becomes more credible when it can identify cases that do not fit a broad version of its own story.

If the fiscal-choke-point argument were simply “platforms are visible, therefore governments make them tax collectors,” the framework would have little analytical value. The current source set contains several internal countercases.

### 5.1 Thailand: intermediary presence is insufficient

Thailand's statutory continuous-process condition limits platform liability. If the platform does not control the relevant sequence from offering through payment and delivery, the existence of platform intermediation alone does not establish the selected platform duty.

This is a direct falsifiability test for generic `platform = collector` coding.

### 5.2 Philippines: payment outside marketplace control

BIR Revenue Memorandum Circular No. 47-2025 supplies a particularly useful within-regime boundary. In the cited scenario, an e-marketplace generates the sale, but payment for the digital service is made directly to the NRDSP account. BIR states that the e-marketplace is not liable for the VAT in that case because payment is outside marketplace control.

This is stronger than a cross-country correlation. Within one legal environment, **marketplace presence does not by itself activate marketplace liability**. Payment/control structure matters.

The Philippines also provides an alternative-node countercase through B2B withholding: the domestic buyer can become the fiscal node even though the service is digitally supplied by a nonresident provider.

### 5.3 Vietnam: platform functionality matters

Vietnam's platform-withholding path is similarly conditional on online-ordering and payment functionality. Seller activity on a platform that lacks the relevant functionality does not support the same platform-withholding classification.

Again, the important variable is not simply whether a platform exists. It is the legal relationship between the platform's operational functions and the fiscal duty.

### 5.4 Indonesia: observability requires legal activation

Indonesia provides a different kind of boundary. Participation in PMSE does not automatically create appointed-collector status. Formal DGT designation activates the special collector role.

The case separates two concepts that are easily conflated:

```text
commercially observable node ≠ legally activated fiscal node
```

### 5.5 What the countercases establish

Together, the countercases strengthen one bounded proposition:

> **The fiscal relevance of a digital intermediary depends on a legally specified relationship between the node's functions and the covered event, not on the label “platform” alone.**

They do not prove that operational control is the sole historical reason legislatures choose a node. Inherited VAT doctrine, international policy diffusion, political feasibility, administrative capability, market concentration, and enforceability remain plausible rival explanations.

## 6. Node locus and event coupling

The positive and boundary cases produce a two-dimensional comparative picture.

### 6.1 Node locus

The same broad digital economy can place responsibility at different nodes:

```text
foreign provider             Malaysia / Philippine B2C
appointed intermediary       Indonesia PMSE VAT
payment-capable platform     Vietnam selected seller path
conditional platform         Thailand selected supplies
buyer / withholding agent    Philippine B2B
qualifying marketplace       Philippine marketplace path
```

This demonstrates that tax-type labels underdescribe the administrative architecture.

### 6.2 Event coupling

The timing of fiscal action also varies:

- Vietnam: transaction/payment-level withholding;
- Indonesia: collection at the commercial event, followed by recurring remittance/reporting;
- Thailand: conditional platform duty inside a periodic VES rail;
- Malaysia: provider registration and periodic return;
- Philippines: different coupling patterns across provider, buyer, and marketplace paths.

Node locus and event coupling are not the same variable. A platform-centered regime can use periodic reporting, while a buyer-centered path can withhold at payment. The distinction prevents the framework from collapsing different administrative choices into a single `platform tax` category.

## 7. From legal architecture to operational fiscal capacity

Assigning a fiscal duty is only the first stage. A legally defined choke point can exist while downstream correction, reconciliation, audit, and dispute mechanisms remain weak, undocumented, or unobserved.

This paper therefore distinguishes a second architecture:

```text
legal node assignment
      ↓
filing / declaration
      ↓
correction / amendment
      ↓
refund / reversal
      ↓
audit / review
      ↓
appeal / dispute
      ↓
enforcement / sanction
      ↓
observed administrative outcome
```

### 7.1 Malaysia

The foreign-provider/service-tax environment exposes filing and adjustment machinery. Customs material documents invoice cancellation, credit/debit-note adjustments, corresponding tax adjustments, and production of cancelled invoices for audit when required. Refund and appeal infrastructure is also publicly available in the wider service-tax framework.

The evidence establishes procedural architecture, not digital-FSP audit yield, refund speed, matching effectiveness, or compliance cost.

### 7.2 Indonesia

The selected PMSE VAT path has strong public evidence for appointment, collection, commercial proof, periodic reporting, and remittance. DGT can also require transaction-detail reporting in the governing administrative framework.

The package does not promote generic Indonesian VAT audit/refund procedure into a PMSE-specific performance claim without instrument-specific evidence. Downstream matching and audit outcomes remain unresolved comparatively.

### 7.3 Vietnam

Decree 117 includes dedicated withholding/declaration schedules and tax-withholding documentation for covered platform seller activity. The legal/administrative framework also includes a refund-request mechanism for households and individuals in the relevant e-commerce setting.

This establishes a correction/refund rail at the legal-mechanism level. It does not establish processing time, error rates, matching quality, or compliance burden.

### 7.4 Thailand

Thailand exposes the deepest public procedural documentation in the selected source pack. Revenue Department e-Service guidance documents registration, filing, refund procedures, supervision/audit of nonresident e-service providers and platforms, electronic appeals, and civil penalties for specified compliance failures.

That is evidence of **public procedural depth**, not evidence that Thailand's system performs better than the others. Source transparency and operational performance are different variables.

### 7.5 Philippines

The VDS system provides a dedicated NRDSP registration, filing, and payment rail. BIR guidance also addresses a documented overpayment scenario: where the NRDSP has already paid VAT and a business buyer also withheld and remitted it, the provider can amend the previously filed digital-services VAT return and carry the overpayment forward under the stated procedure.

Again, the mechanism demonstrates error-correction architecture, not its empirical effectiveness.

### 7.6 Cross-case implication

The Stage-B comparison supports one negative and one positive conclusion.

The negative conclusion is that **formal law cannot be used as a proxy for operational fiscal capacity**.

The positive conclusion is that digital-tax systems can be compared along downstream procedural dimensions where official evidence actually exists. Filing, correction, refund, audit, appeal, and enforcement can be coded separately without converting them into a scalar maturity score.

A future performance study would require comparable outcome data: filing timeliness, unmatched transactions, refund counts and processing time, audit selection and yield, penalties, appeals, compliance costs, seller coverage, and behavioral displacement.

## 8. Fiscal capacity and platform governance

### 8.1 Administrative distance

Digital commerce can create a large enforcement surface: nonresident suppliers, millions of sellers, fragmented transactions, and limited physical presence. Assigning bounded duties to transaction-organizing actors can reduce the number of direct administrative relationships required for specified obligations.

This is best understood as a reduction in **administrative distance**, not the creation of complete visibility.

Information can be inaccurate. Transactions can occur off-platform. Sellers can fall below thresholds or outside scope. Buyer location can be ambiguous. Refunds and cancellations can reverse the economic event. Platforms can lack relevant control functions. An intermediary can hold data that the state is not legally authorized to obtain.

### 8.2 Delegation costs

Intermediation also creates governance costs. Compliance duties can be passed to platforms and sellers. Data retention and reporting create privacy and security risks. Errors create due-process and correction problems. Concentrating public functions in dominant platforms can reinforce private market power or create dependence on private infrastructure.

The relevant policy question is therefore not “should governments use platforms?” in the abstract. It is:

> **Which actor should perform which fiscal function for which transaction class, subject to what information, correction, and accountability constraints?**

### 8.3 Architecture before performance

The framework suggests a sequencing rule for empirical research.

Before estimating whether a rate or regime affects revenue, researchers should map the path through which liability becomes administrable. Only then can outcome variation be interpreted without confusing market growth, legal change, remitter population, base change, and enforcement intensity.

This is why the present paper does not resurrect the inherited ASEAN rate panel. A future causal design should preferably exploit within-country institutional transitions where the legal object is stable and the node or rail changes observably over time.

## 9. Limitations and identification frontier

The current study has six main limitations.

First, the cases are purposive and cannot establish regional prevalence.

Second, the analysis identifies legal and procedural architectures, not the historical causal determinants of node selection. International template diffusion, tax doctrine, administrative capacity, market structure, political feasibility, and enforcement constraints remain rival explanations.

Third, public documentation is uneven. More detailed guidance can make one country appear procedurally richer even if actual performance is not better.

Fourth, comparable operational outcomes remain scarce. Aggregate collection totals are not substitutes for matching, error, refund, audit, or compliance-cost data.

Fifth, the framework does not provide a welfare model. Reducing administrative distance can coexist with higher private compliance cost, privacy concerns, market-power effects, or seller exit.

Sixth, legal sources change. The package is currentness-controlled through September 2026, but a submission version requires a final legal sweep immediately before filing.

The strongest next empirical designs are likely within-country changes: appointment waves, shifts from seller/provider remittance to platform withholding, changes in payment-control requirements, threshold reforms, or introduction of transaction-detail reporting. Such events could support interrupted time-series, phased-adoption, or difference-in-differences analysis only where treatment timing, comparison groups, and outcomes are genuinely defensible.

## 10. Conclusion

Digital taxation is not only a problem of rates and bases. It is a problem of institutional placement.

Malaysia, Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, and the Philippines assign fiscal functions to different nodes inside digital transaction paths. Providers, appointed collectors, payment-capable platforms, conditional platforms, buyers, and marketplaces can all become responsible under different legal conditions.

The comparison reveals that the label `platform tax` is too coarse. Responsibility depends on the legal object, node, nexus evidence, transaction rail, and record relationship. It also depends on **activation conditions**: payment control, continuous-process control, transaction class, thresholds, and formal designation can determine whether a visible intermediary becomes a fiscal node at all.

The boundary cases are therefore as important as the positive cases. Thailand, the Philippines, Vietnam, and Indonesia each show in different ways that commercial intermediation does not automatically create the selected fiscal duty.

The second-stage analysis adds another boundary: **legal assignment is not operational performance**. Filing, amendment, refund, audit, appeal, enforcement, and measurable outcomes are separate stages. Some are well documented in the current public sources; many performance outcomes remain unknown.

The fiscal-choke-point concept is consequently best understood as a transaction-level institutional map, not a theory that platforms are inherently optimal tax collectors. Its contribution is to make one prior question explicit:

> **Where, under what legal and operational conditions, can public fiscal responsibility be attached to private transaction infrastructure?**

Answering that question cleanly creates a better foundation for the harder causal question that follows: whether a particular node and rail actually improve compliance, revenue, equity, or administrative efficiency.

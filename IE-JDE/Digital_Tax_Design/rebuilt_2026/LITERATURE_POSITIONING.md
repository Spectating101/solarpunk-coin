# Digital Tax 2026 — Literature Positioning

This note fixes the novelty claim for the rebuilt *Fiscal Choke Points* paper. It is not a conventional literature review draft; it is a control document for what the manuscript may claim as inherited, synthesized, or genuinely new.

## 1. What is already established

### A. Firms as fiscal intermediaries

Kleven, Kreiner, and Saez (2016) explain how firms with accurate internal records can become effective third-party reporters and thereby relax tax-enforcement constraints. The core idea that governments can use record-keeping organizations as fiscal intermediaries therefore predates this paper.

**Implication for Digital Tax:** do not claim that assigning fiscal duties to information-rich private actors is novel.

### B. VAT paper trails and third-party information

Pomeranz (2015) provides experimental evidence that VAT paper trails create preventive deterrence and enforcement spillovers. Transaction records can therefore function as an enforcement technology, not merely as bookkeeping.

**Implication for Digital Tax:** do not claim that transaction records or reconciliation are newly discovered sources of fiscal capacity.

### C. Platform VAT/GST liability

OECD (2019) explicitly analyzes regimes in which digital platforms can be made fully or partially liable for assessing, collecting, and remitting VAT/GST on online sales. A central policy rationale is to reduce the cost and risk of administering large volumes of online transactions by concentrating obligations on a relatively limited number of platforms.

**Implication for Digital Tax:** platform collection/liability is direct prior art. `Fiscal choke point` must not be sold as a new tax mechanism.

### D. Administration of imported digital services and low-value goods

Brondolo (2021) and the OECD/WBG/ADB Asia-Pacific VAT Digital Toolkit (2022) address the practical architecture needed to implement VAT on imported digital services and e-commerce. These sources cover registration, simplified compliance, vendor collection, platform roles, administration, and implementation design.

**Implication for Digital Tax:** the paper belongs inside tax-administration scholarship, not outside it as a discovery of digital taxation.

### E. Platforms as an administrative opportunity

Aslam and Shah (2017) note that peer-to-peer platforms intensify familiar small-business tax challenges while also creating a technological opportunity to improve tax administration because platforms record transactions and participant activity.

**Implication for Digital Tax:** the link between platform data and fragmented-taxpayer administration is also established.

### F. ASEAN digital-tax comparison is also prior art

The Philippines National Tax Research Center has already compared VAT and VAT-like taxation of digital transactions across ASEAN member states, emphasizing variation in rates, tax structures, and collection methods. Comparative legal scholarship has likewise examined Indonesia alongside other ASEAN regimes, including work focused on OTT services, nexus, and VAT/CIT treatment.

**Implication for Digital Tax:** do not claim that this is the first ASEAN digital-tax comparison. The contribution must survive after conceding that regional comparative mapping already exists.

## 2. What the paper contributes

The defensible contribution is a **specific comparative institutional architecture for transaction-node responsibility**, applied to five Southeast Asian digital-tax regimes.

The paper makes three linked contributions.

### Contribution 1 — a different unit of comparison

Existing regional comparisons commonly organize regimes by statutory tax type, rate, threshold, nexus rule, or country policy. *Fiscal Choke Points* instead compares the institutional connection between:

```text
commercial event
→ taxable object
→ liable node
→ destination evidence
→ transaction / filing rail
→ bounded fiscal action
```

The term **fiscal choke point** names that connection. It is a comparative construct, not a claim that the underlying tax instrument is new and not a claim that cross-country ASEAN comparison is itself new.

### Contribution 2 — a five-component architecture

The paper operationalizes each jurisdiction through five components:

1. taxable object;
2. liable node;
3. destination evidence;
4. transaction rail;
5. reconciliation power.

This allows unlike tax instruments to be compared without pretending that their revenue measures, tax bases, or legal duties are identical.

### Contribution 3 — node locus and event coupling as institutional variables

Across the five cases, governments place fiscal responsibility at different nodes: foreign supplier, appointed collector, payment-capable platform, platform satisfying a continuous-process test, or domestic buyer/withholding agent. The comparative question is therefore not only *what rate applies?* but *which actor is legally activated, and how closely is the fiscal duty coupled to the transaction event?*

This is the strongest route to originality. The paper should show that the **locus and timing of fiscal responsibility are institutional design choices that can be compared across legally heterogeneous regimes**.

## 3. The novelty sentence to defend

Use wording in this territory:

> This paper does not introduce platform tax intermediation or ASEAN digital-tax comparison as new. It contributes a transaction-node framework for identifying where and how heterogeneous digital-tax regimes activate fiscal responsibility inside private commercial infrastructure, and applies that framework consistently across five Southeast Asian jurisdictions.

Avoid wording in this territory:

> Governments are newly discovering how to use platforms as tax collectors.

or:

> Fiscal choke points are a new form of taxation invented in Southeast Asia.

or:

> This is the first comparative study of digital taxation in ASEAN.

## 4. Comparative findings the prior literature does not supply automatically

The country evidence should be used to establish bounded cross-case findings rather than to repeat country descriptions.

### Finding A — fiscal responsibility follows control functions, not one legal form

The cases place obligations on actors that control some combination of customer location, payment, invoicing, transaction confirmation, or market access. The legal form differs, but the assignment repeatedly follows operational control.

### Finding B — event coupling varies

The regimes differ in how closely the fiscal obligation is coupled to the commercial event.

- **Transaction-coupled:** Vietnam links withholding to successful transaction confirmation and payment acceptance.
- **Collection-coupled:** Indonesia assigns collection and proof-of-VAT duties to appointed PMSE collectors.
- **Periodic/provider-centered:** Malaysia primarily operates through foreign-provider registration and periodic remittance.
- **Conditional platform/provider rail:** Thailand shifts responsibility toward the platform when the continuous offer-payment-delivery condition is satisfied.
- **Split by transaction type:** the Philippines uses provider remittance for covered B2C transactions and buyer/withholding-agent routes for specified B2B transactions.

This is a typology, not a ranking.

### Finding C — concentrated intermediation reduces administrative distance but does not create total visibility

The cases show a move from dispersed taxpayers toward fewer legally activated nodes for specified obligations. That can reduce administrative distance. It does not establish complete information, optimal collection, low compliance cost, or absence of privacy and governance risks.

## 5. Literature-facing research gap

The paper should frame the gap as follows:

1. tax-administration theory explains why third-party records and intermediaries matter;
2. VAT/platform guidance explains how platforms can be assigned collection or reporting duties;
3. ASEAN comparative work already maps differences in rates, structures, nexus, and legal treatment;
4. individual country rules show how specific mechanisms are implemented;
5. **the remaining contribution is a compact transaction-node architecture that preserves instrument heterogeneity while making the locus and transaction timing of fiscal responsibility directly comparable across the five cases.**

That is a narrower contribution than “new theory of digital tax,” but it is much more defensible.

## 6. Required manuscript literature set

At minimum, the manuscript should engage:

- Kleven, Kreiner & Saez (2016) — firms as fiscal intermediaries;
- Pomeranz (2015) — VAT information trails;
- OECD (2019) — platform VAT/GST liability;
- Brondolo (2021) — administration of imported digital services/LVG;
- OECD/WBG/ADB (2022) — Asia-Pacific implementation framework;
- Aslam & Shah (2017) — platform technology and P2P tax administration;
- Keen & Slemrod (2017) — tax-administration design.

The research-control package should additionally track at least one prior ASEAN-wide government comparison and one prior ASEAN comparative legal study so the paper cannot accidentally imply that regional comparison itself is novel:

- Philippines NTRC, *VAT and VAT-like Taxation of Digital Transactions and Services in ASEAN*;
- Sugeng, Aidy & Cardenas Jr. (2025), comparative OTT/digital-tax regulation in Indonesia and ASEAN.

These regional-comparison sources are novelty controls. Country-law facts in the manuscript should still be grounded in the frozen primary sources rather than in secondary regional summaries.

## 7. Hostile-review test

A reviewer should be able to say:

> I already knew platforms could collect VAT, third-party information improves enforcement, and ASEAN digital-tax regimes have been compared before. What this paper adds is a disciplined way to compare which transaction node is legally activated, through which rail, and how tightly the fiscal duty is coupled to the commercial event across five different regimes.

If the paper cannot elicit that reaction, the novelty claim needs further work.

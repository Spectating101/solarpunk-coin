# Institutional Discovery and Pilot Protocol

**Status:** active operating protocol  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 2026-08-07  
**Issues:** #29, #33  
**Authority:** subordinate to `docs/project/MAXIMUM_VALUE_EXECUTION_PROGRAM.md`, `submission/PROGRAMME_SCOREBOARD.md`, and P4.

This protocol tests whether a bounded programme output deserves institutional adoption, paid delivery, licensing, grant-backed deployment, or a company. It does not presume that commercialization is the programme’s final destination.

---

## 1. Separate participant roles

Every contact must be classified by actual role:

| Role | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Source partner | can provide attributable data or a workflow artifact |
| Operator | performs or supervises the current process |
| User | would inspect or act on the output |
| Influencer | advises the decision but does not control budget |
| Buyer | can approve a purchase or formal pilot |
| Budget owner | controls the relevant budget line |
| Gatekeeper | legal, security, privacy, procurement, or institutional authority |
| Channel partner | can distribute or integrate the product to other organizations |
| Reviewer | provides domain or technical scrutiny rather than demand evidence |

One person may hold several roles, but the evidence for each role must be recorded separately.

---

## 2. Commercialization hypotheses

Test independently:

- **H1 — Evidence-readiness service:** early diagnosis and reproducible case package.
- **H2 — Institutional policy engine:** versioned rules, binding constraints, and repeatable decisions.
- **H3 — Licensed component:** evidence, policy, receipt, or conformance functions embedded in another platform.
- **H4 — Grant-backed open infrastructure:** cases, benchmark, reference implementation, research, and interoperability funded as public-interest infrastructure.

Do not aggregate weak interest across hypotheses into a general “market validation” claim.

---

## 3. Interview entry condition

Broad interviews begin after at least one of these exists:

- an inspectable external-case artifact;
- a precise source-dependent blocker;
- a stable demonstration tied to exact controlled evidence;
- a non-confidential workflow brief.

This prevents interviews from becoming reactions to vague architecture language.

---

## 4. Interview structure

### Context

1. What organization and role are represented?
2. What evidence-dependent decision or claim do they currently make?
3. Which systems, files, people, and authorities participate?
4. How often does the workflow occur?

### Current process

5. Where does source data originate?
6. How are timestamps, units, intervals, and ownership/custody established?
7. Who decides whether evidence is sufficient?
8. What software, consultant, spreadsheet, auditor, or internal procedure is used?
9. What artifact records the final decision?

### Failure and consequence

10. What is commonly missing, inconsistent, late, or disputed?
11. When are problems discovered?
12. What happens after a blocked, rejected, or corrected case?
13. What staff time, delay, rework, financial exposure, or reputational risk follows?
14. Which errors matter enough to justify a budget?

### Value and alternatives

15. Would an earlier blocked or bounded result change an action?
16. What output would be trusted: memo, machine report, receipt, API, review record, or integration?
17. What existing alternative is “good enough”?
18. What would make switching or adding a new layer unacceptable?
19. Is the need one-time, periodic, continuous, or event-driven?

### Authority and budget

20. Who owns the problem?
21. Who can authorize data access?
22. Who approves pilots and purchases?
23. Which budget line would apply?
24. What procurement, privacy, security, or legal gate would block adoption?

### Evidence commitment

25. Will they provide a bounded source sample?
26. Will they review factual metadata?
27. Will they introduce the relevant operator or buyer?
28. Will they provide a written expression of interest?
29. Will they evaluate a scoped pilot or quotation?
30. Will they pay, contribute staff time, host a deployment, or fund research?

General praise without a concrete commitment remains qualitative feedback.

## 5. Evidence strength

| Evidence class | Example | What it supports |
|---|---|---|
| `INTEREST` | positive conversation | communication relevance only |
| `PROBLEM CONFIRMATION` | role describes current failure and consequence | problem existence for that context |
| `RESOURCE COMMITMENT` | data, staff time, reviewer time, or introduction | willingness to participate |
| `FORMAL INTEREST` | signed or attributable LOI / pilot note | procurement or partnership exploration |
| `BUDGET CONFIRMATION` | identified budget owner and plausible amount | commercial feasibility for that buyer |
| `PAID PILOT` | signed scope and payment evidence | willingness to pay for a defined result |
| `REPEAT USE` | second engagement or continued workflow | early repeatability |
| `RECURRING CONTRACT` | ongoing paid service | recurring demand |

Do not promote one class into another.

---

## 6. Interview counting rules

A counted interview requires:

- attributable or properly pseudonymized participant record;
- role and organization type;
- date and interviewer;
- hypothesis tested;
- current-process evidence;
- problem and consequence;
- alternatives;
- authority and budget information where known;
- commitments and next action;
- permission for quotations or public use.

Do not count:

- repeated conversations with the same person as new organizations;
- casual praise;
- competition judges without workflow evidence;
- the founder’s own assumptions;
- source-holder factual review as customer discovery unless buyer questions are also addressed.

---

## 7. Pilot specification

Every pilot must freeze:

- pilot ID and version;
- organization and buyer role;
- problem statement;
- source and permission contract;
- input boundaries;
- exact deliverables;
- success criteria;
- valid blocked/failure outcome;
- timeline and responsibilities;
- price, funded resource, or explicit no-fee exchange;
- publication and testimonial rights;
- data retention and deletion;
- support and liability boundary;
- intellectual-property treatment;
- post-pilot decision.

### Minimum valid success criteria

A pilot may succeed when:

- the workflow processes the authorized artifact set;
- diagnostics and unresolved semantics are explicit;
- the declared policy produces a reproducible result;
- the operator understands the blocker or limit;
- the result changes or clarifies a decision;
- the customer can reproduce or verify the authorized package.

A positive admission result is not required.

---

## 8. Free and discounted work

A no-fee or discounted engagement must purchase specific validation value:

- case-publication rights;
- attributable testimonial;
- structured operator feedback;
- permission to name the organization type or identity;
- introduction to a buyer or partner;
- repeat-use option;
- access to a materially different source environment.

Record standard price, discount, consideration received, and future-price boundary. Do not normalize indefinite free implementation.

---

## 9. Decision thresholds

### Continue a hypothesis when

- at least three relevant participants independently confirm the same material problem;
- at least one qualified organization commits data, time, authority, or budget;
- the output changes a real decision or reduces measurable effort/risk;
- a reusable workflow emerges.

### Prepare a paid pilot when

- the buyer and budget owner are identified;
- scope and success criteria are clear;
- data and permission can be obtained;
- delivery effort can be estimated;
- IP, privacy, publication, and liability boundaries are acceptable.

### Build recurring software when

- three organizations request repeated processing;
- their workflows share a stable core;
- onboarding can be standardized;
- continuing access is worth an annual budget;
- support and security obligations are sustainable.

### Stop or change the hypothesis when

- 20 relevant interviews produce fewer than three serious confirmations;
- no qualified organization allocates data, time, authority, or budget;
- every case remains bespoke with no reusable core;
- existing tools or consultants solve the problem at lower total switching cost;
- legal or institutional restrictions eliminate the buyer;
- commercialization work weakens the research programme without generating equivalent evidence.

---

## 10. Institutional-use gate

M5 requires more than interviews:

- three independent organizations complete a bounded workflow;
- one repeats the workflow;
- one external operator completes a documented path without founder intervention at every step;
- attributable feedback produces corrections;
- the organization’s role and limits are recorded;
- no use is described as adoption beyond the documented scope.

---

## 11. Commercial-proof gate

M6 requires:

- one paid institutional pilot;
- three serious written expressions of interest or equivalent procurement evidence;
- one defined initial buyer and budget owner;
- repeatable problem, deliverable, success criteria, and price;
- ownership and licence treatment;
- evidence that value comes from the result rather than support for a student project.

---

## 12. Reporting

The programme scoreboard should report:

- interviews by role and hypothesis;
- distinct organizations;
- problem confirmations;
- resource commitments;
- formal interest;
- pilots proposed, accepted, completed, paid, repeated, or rejected;
- onboarding and delivery effort;
- reasons for rejection;
- stop or pivot decisions.

It must not report a broad total addressable market as evidence of reachable demand.
